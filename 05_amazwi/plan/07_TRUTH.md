## 1. THE NAME — a collision you must know about before a judge tells you

**"Amazwi" is the name of a South African national museum.**

The National English Literary Museum in Makhanda was renamed **Amazwi South African Museum of Literature** in May 2019 by order of the Minister of Arts and Culture. *Amazwi* — "voices" or "words" — was chosen to reflect "linguistic and cultural inclusivity," and the institution's mandate is to collect and promote literature **in all of South Africa's official languages**.
*Source: [amazwi.museum](https://www.amazwi.museum/) · [LitNet press release](https://www.litnet.co.za/press-release-nelm-rebrands-as-amazwi-south-african-museum-of-literature/)*

### How serious is this?
- **Trademark risk: low.** Museum services and cultural heritage is a different class from entertainment software and financial services. Direct infringement is unlikely.
- **Reputational risk: real.** A judge from the cultural sector will recognise the name immediately. Appearing not to know is careless in a product whose entire premise is cultural respect.

### The recommendation: keep the name, own the fact, convert it
Renaming two days out costs more than it saves, and the name is already in your drafts. **But you must never be told this by someone else.** Have the line ready and deliver it as a strength:

> *"Amazwi is also the name of South Africa's national museum of literature — which collects writing in all twelve official languages. We know. We think that's exactly the right neighbourhood to be standing in, and they are the first partnership call we would make."*

That answer turns a landmine into evidence you did your homework. A partnership with a national institution whose mandate is literally multilingual preservation would be an extraordinary credibility asset.

### If you would rather not carry it — the alternatives, ranked
| Name | Meaning | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| **ZWAKALA** | *be heard · be audible* (Nguni) — also current SA slang for *"come through"* | An imperative, energetic, youth-native, and it encodes the product: make yourself heard. The strongest alternative. |
| **MAMELA** | *listen* (isiZulu/isiXhosa imperative) | Names the novel half of the product — the guessing side. Warm and unmistakably local. |
| **UMLOZI** | *the whistle* | Currently the core game-mode name. Could be promoted. |
| **IZWI** | *voice*, singular | Cleaner, no collision, but less collective. |

Decide today or not at all. Do not relitigate this on Wednesday.

**❌ "Nobody has thought of using a game to collect speech data."**
False, and a linguistics-literate judge will know. **Games With A Purpose for language resources is an established research area** with a twenty-year literature — the ESP Game, GWAPs for part-of-speech tagging, serious games for speech-emotion data, a GWAP that collected Arabic transcriptions.

## 3. THE COMPETITOR PROBLEM — handle these three before they handle you

### 3.1 African Next Voices / Swivuriso — the real one
This is closer to AMAZWI than the earlier project documents assumed, and you must not be surprised by it.

ANV is a consortium — DSFSI at the University of Pretoria, Maseno (Kenya), Data Science Nigeria, RobotsMali, with Masakhane, Lelapa AI and Mozilla Common Voice as partners — funded by a **$2.2m Gates Foundation grant plus Meta funding**. It covers **seven South African languages**, the same ones you would target. It explicitly commits to *"informed consent, fair compensation, and clear data-rights terms."* Its South African subset **prohibits TTS, voice cloning and voice synthesis uses**. Its sister work, the **Esethu Framework**, has already published a community-centric licence for **equitable benefit-sharing**.

**Do not compete with this. Build on it, and say so.**

> *"Swivuriso and African Next Voices are the best thing that has happened to South African speech data, and we seed from them. They're a funded research programme producing a dataset release — it has a beginning and an end, it's collected through university fieldwork in specific districts, and there's no way for an ordinary person to open something on a Tuesday and contribute. We're the continuous consumer layer on top: a game, a payment rail, and per-contributor consent lineage. And the licence we implement is theirs — the Esethu Framework proposed equitable benefit-sharing academically; we'd be the first to actually run it at consumer scale with a mobile money payout."*

That answer is generous, accurate, credits South African researchers, and is far stronger than pretending they don't exist. **It also makes the DSFSI relationship your most obvious partnership ask.**

### 3.2 Duolingo Immersion and Incubator — the sharpest precedent
Duolingo's original business model *was* crowdsourced work funding a free product. **Immersion** had learners translate real documents which Duolingo sold to clients; it was shut down at the end of 2013 because, in Duolingo's own account, it *"added unnecessary complexity and didn't improve the core learning experience."* The **Incubator** for volunteer course-building was later frozen and retired too.

**The answer, and it must be precise:**
> *"Duolingo's crowdsourcing was translating text to avoid charging users, and it was a bolt-on to the lesson. They killed it because it didn't improve the learning. Ours is the opposite on all three counts: it's native spoken audio, not text; the money flows outward to contributors rather than inward to subsidise a free product; and the guessing **is** the lesson. Comprehending a real native speaker at speed is not a tax on learning — it's the highest-value form of it. If the game stopped teaching, we'd have the same problem they had. It doesn't."*

### 3.3 The exploitation comparison — raise it yourself
The first thing a sceptical judge will reach for is **Sama/OpenAI in Kenya** ($1.32–2/hr against $12.50/hr billing) and **Remotasks Kenya** (shut down March 2024, Fairwork 1/10 on fair pay). **Also note the 2026 headwind:** Toloka pivoted away from the open crowd in July 2026, a signal that mass low-skill data work is becoming commercially weaker relative to expert data.

Raise all of it before they do. Your structural differences: rates published before the task and never cut retroactively; consent versioned and revocable; contributors credited by name; adults only; a game designed to be worth playing with the money switched off. **And your asset is scarce, not commoditised** — South African conversational speech is not generic annotation labour.

## 4. LEGAL

### 4.1 The game mechanic and trade marks
***30 Seconds* is a registered trade mark of a South African company (Calco Games), created by Calie Esterhuyse and published in 1998.**

- ✅ **Game mechanics are not protectable.** Describe-a-word-against-a-timer cannot be owned.
- ❌ **The name, card content and trade dress are.** Never brand anything "30 Seconds". Never reproduce their cards. Never imitate the box.
- ✅ In the pitch, reference it as a cultural touchstone: *"the describe-it-without-saying-it game every South African has played."*
- ⚠️ **Do not put "30 Seconds" in the submitted form** as a product descriptor.

### 4.2 POPIA
Voice recordings are personal information. **Voice used for identification is biometric — special personal information with a materially higher burden.** This is why the architecture does not authenticate on voice (`02_TECH.md` §7.2), and the decision is defensible as both engineering and law.

Controls: adults only · versioned granular consent · purpose limitation enforced in code · no precise location · no stored identity documents · consent checked again at export · revocation blocks future use · full audit trail.

**The honest limit, stated plainly:** you cannot un-train a model that has already seen the data. Handle it with a declared retirement and retraining policy. Do not promise instant unlearning.

### 4.3 🔴 Promotional competitions — the statute, corrected

An earlier version of this document named the **Lotteries Act** and the **National Gambling Act**. **That is the wrong statute.**

> **Promotional competitions in South Africa have been governed by the Consumer Protection Act 68 of 2008, section 36 — not the Lotteries Act — since April 2011**, read with regulation 11 of the CPA Regulations (GNR.293, 1 April 2011). The National Lotteries Commission still monitors compliance.

**The definition, and the two limbs:**
> *"any competition, game, scheme, arrangement, system, plan or device for **distributing prizes by lot or chance**"* — **if** it is conducted in the ordinary course of business **for the purpose of promoting** a producer, supplier, or the sale of goods or services, **and** any prize exceeds the prescribed threshold.

- **The threshold is R1.00.** Effectively every competition is caught.
- **"Prize" is broad:** a reward, gift, free good or service, price reduction or concession.
- **No consideration** may be required from a participant beyond reasonable transmission cost, capped at **R1.50** electronically.
- ⚠️ **And the trap:** SA legal commentary warns the definition catches competitions **regardless of whether skill is required.** You cannot escape s36 by bolting a skill element onto a prize draw. My earlier "it's skill-based, so we're fine" reasoning was too quick.

### Where AMAZWI actually sits
*Not legal advice — this is the analysis to take to a lawyer, not a substitute for one.*

| Element | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **Per-contribution payments (R2.00)** | **Very likely outside s36.** This is *consideration for a service rendered* — you recorded something, it was validated — not a prize distributed by lot or chance to promote goods. A different legal character entirely, closer to payment for work |
| **Leaderboards and leagues** | 🔴 **The actual exposure.** If any prize attaches — and "prize" includes a free good, service or concession above R1 — and it promotes the business, s36 is arguably engaged |
| **Any randomised mechanic** | **Squarely inside.** Already excluded from the design |

**The five rules that follow** — all of which we were already doing, now for a better reason:
1. **Leagues award non-cash points and status only. No prizes, ever.** The wall between points and money in `01_PRODUCT.md` §5 now has a *legal* justification, not just a product one.
2. **No chance-based mechanics anywhere.**
3. **Never require payment to participate.**
4. **Publish the rules; never cut a published rate retroactively.**
5. **Get a formal opinion before any real-money launch.** The regulator is actively tightening — see reporting on increased scrutiny of till-slip and "buy-and-win" competitions, May 2026.

**The answer if a judge asks:**
> *"Our payments are consideration for validated work, not prizes distributed by chance, so we read them as outside CPA section 36 — which is the statute that actually governs promotional competitions here, not the Lotteries Act. That's also why our leagues award points and status only, never prizes, and why there's no randomised mechanic anywhere in the product. We'd take a formal opinion before a commercial launch."*

Full analysis and sources: `research/F_GAMIFICATION.md` §9.

## 6. THE ETHICS POSITION

State this on a slide, unprompted, before anyone asks.

1. **Adults only.** Voice is personal information; children's data carries additional restrictions.
2. **Consent is versioned, granular, in the contributor's own language, and revocable.** Each item independently declinable.
3. **Rates are published before the task and never reduced afterwards.** Retroactive rate cuts are the industry's characteristic betrayal.
4. **Contributors retain rights** and grant a specific, purpose-limited, revocable licence. They are credited by name in the Archive.
5. **No biometric authentication.** Voice is the interface, never the lock.
6. **Coverage pricing, never ethnicity pricing.**
7. **The game is worth playing with the money off.** Tested, not asserted.
8. **Endangered languages are a protected tier, not a market rate.** Their value is not commercial and the pricing must not pretend it is.
9. **We implement someone else's ethics framework and credit them** — the Esethu Framework from DSFSI — rather than inventing our own and claiming novelty.

> The closing thought for the ethics slide:
> **"The question isn't whether South African voices are valuable. Every AI company has already answered that. The question is who gets paid."**
