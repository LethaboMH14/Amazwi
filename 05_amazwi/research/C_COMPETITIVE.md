## 1. Paid voice/data work

**Karya (India)** is the single closest analogue to AMAZWI's *payment* model and is the project most worth studying in depth: a nonprofit that pays rural/marginalised Indian workers roughly $5/hour for voice-recording tasks (~20x local minimum wage), caps lifetime earnings around $1,500/worker to keep it framed as supplemental rather than exploitative-permanent income, and — distinctively — gives workers de-facto ownership of the data so that resale royalties flow back to them. It has scaled to 35,000+ active workers across 24 Indian states with ~100,000 more workers "ready," and in 2024-2025 signed contracts with Microsoft Research, Google DeepMind, OpenAI, Anthropic, and the Government of India. Its 2025 "Samiksha" initiative (public, community-led AI evaluation, 20,000+ real-world queries) has a paper accepted to CHI 2026. Karya is *not* gamified and is *not* a consumer product — it is a task-marketplace app targeted at people who need income, not people who want entertainment. [karya.in](https://www.karya.in/), [reports.karya.in](https://reports.karya.in/)

**Sama, Appen, Scale AI/Remotasks, Toloka, Clickworker, Surge AI** are the commercial data-labeling/RLHF industry AMAZWI is adjacent to but not competing with directly — they buy generic labeled data/human feedback for AI labs at industrial scale, not community speech corpora. Pay ranges wildly by geography and platform:
- Sama/OpenAI Kenya: workers paid $1.32-$2/hr take-home despite OpenAI paying Sama $12.50/hr per worker — this is the industry's most cited exploitation scandal, and led to 150+ workers unionizing. [TIME](https://time.com/6247678/openai-chatgpt-kenya-workers/), [CBS](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/ai-work-kenya-exploitation-60-minutes/)
- Scale AI/Remotasks: Oxford's Fairwork project scored Remotasks 1/10 on fair-pay criteria; documented cases of a worker paid $0.30 for 4 hours and payments withheld or delayed. [Business & Human Rights Centre](https://www.business-humanrights.org/en/latest-news/philippines-scale-ai-creating-race-to-the-bottom-as-outsourced-workers-face-poor-conditions-in-digital-sweatshops-incl-low-wages-withheld-payments/)
- Toloka, Clickworker, Surge AI/DataAnnotation pay better on paper ($8-60/hr depending on region/specialty) but are general-purpose gig marketplaces with no language-preservation or corpus-building mission, and no South African language focus found.

The controversy pattern across this whole category — underpayment relative to what clients pay, payment delays/withholding, psychological harm from disturbing content, precarity (no benefits, no guaranteed work) — is the reputational risk AMAZWI must visibly avoid, and its MoMo micropayment-per-validated-recording model is structurally closer to Karya's per-task fair-wage model than to the Sama/Scale exploitation pattern.

## 2. Open speech collection

**Mozilla Common Voice**: nonprofit, CC0, the largest fully-open multilingual voice dataset. As of the most recent releases found (mid-2026), it spans 286-294 languages and roughly 35,900 total hours / ~24,600 validated hours. It does *not* pay individual contributors per recording; instead it ran an "Open Multilingual Speech Fund" (grants to organisations, applications through March 2025) and community-organized "contribute-a-thons." Mozilla is piloting more-restrictive licensing terms specifically with African language communities (led by Dr Lilian Wanzare, Nairobi) in response to contributor concerns about their voices being used commercially without benefit — a direct precedent for why AMAZWI's consent/compensation framing matters. South African languages (isiNdebele, Sesotho, etc.) are present in Common Voice but per-language SA hour counts could not be confirmed from the language-stats page (it returned no populated data on fetch). The parent Mozilla Foundation cut 30% of staff in November 2024 (advocacy/global-programs divisions), but Common Voice itself kept shipping releases through 2025-2026, so it appears organisationally de-prioritized but not abandoned. [mozillafoundation.org](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/en/blog/latest-common-voice-dataset-surpasses-20000-hours-of-open-source-speech-data/), [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/11/05/mozilla-foundation-lays-off-30-staff-drops-advocacy-division/)

**Google WAXAL — resolving the conflicting figures.** The task specifically asked to resolve "11,000 hours / 27 languages" vs "~1,846h ASR + 565h TTS." Having pulled Google's own PR blog (blog.google/intl/en-africa), Google's research blog (research.google/blog), the arXiv paper (2602.02734, v1 Feb 2 2026 / v3 Mar 2 2026), and the Hugging Face dataset card (google/WaxalNLP) directly, **the figures do not resolve to one number — they are inconsistent even across Google's own official channels**, not merely between press coverage and a paper:
- Google's Africa press blog: "over 11,000 hours of speech data from nearly 2 million individual recordings," naming 21-22 languages (varying lists cited: Acholi, Akan, Dagaare, Dagbani, Dholuo, Ewe, Fante, Fulani/Fula, Hausa, Igbo, Ikposo/Kposo, Kikuyu, Lingala, Luganda, Malagasy, Masaaba, Nyankole, Rukiga, Shona, Soga/Lusoga, Swahili, Yoruba), launch dated Feb 2, 2026.
- Google Research's technical blog and the arXiv paper/dataset card converge on a much smaller *curated/released* figure: **~1,250 hours of transcribed ASR speech + ~180-235 hours of studio TTS speech (≈1,430-1,485 hours total)**, across 19 ASR languages and 17 TTS languages (with overlap) — including Amharic, Oromo, Sidama, Tigrinya, Wolaytta not mentioned in the press list.
- Third-party press coverage (Rest of World, TechAfrica News, UrbanGeekz) repeats "27 native African languages, 100M+ speakers, 26+ countries" — a number that appears nowhere in the technical sources and may be a rounding/press-amplification artifact of an even earlier or later count.
- **Most plausible resolution** (not confirmed by an explicit Google statement, but consistent with the pattern): "11,000 hours / ~2 million recordings" is *raw collected audio before quality filtering*, while "~1,430-2,411 hours" is the *cleaned, QC'd, publicly released* dataset — a very large raw-to-released attrition ratio (roughly 85-90% of raw audio discarded), which if true is itself a notable data point about how much "wild" contributed speech fails to survive quality control.
- **South African language check: confirmed negative.** Across every source fetched — the press release, the research blog, the arXiv paper, and the HF dataset card — **no South African official language (isiZulu, isiXhosa, Afrikaans, Sesotho, Setswana, Sepedi, Xitsonga, Tshivenda, siSwati, isiNdebele) appears anywhere in WAXAL's language list.** WAXAL's partner institutions (Makerere/Uganda, University of Ghana, Digital Umuganda/Rwanda, Addis Ababa University/Ethiopia, AIMS Senegal, Media Trust/Nigeria) are all outside South Africa. [research.google](https://research.google/blog/waxal-a-large-scale-open-resource-for-african-language-speech-technology/), [blog.google](https://blog.google/intl/en-africa/company-news/outreach-and-initiatives/introducing-waxal-a-new-open-dataset-for-african-speech-technology/), [arXiv 2602.02734](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02734), [HF google/WaxalNLP](https://huggingface.co/datasets/google/WaxalNLP)

**NVIDIA Granary** (Aug 15, 2025): ~1 million hours (650k ASR + 350k translation) across 25 European languages — huge scale, but Europe-only and machine-assisted, not a human-paid-contribution model or an African/SA project. [NVIDIA blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-and-mozilla-release-common-voice-dataset-surpassing-13000-hours-for-the-first-time/)

**Bhashini (India, govt.)**: public digital infrastructure since 2022, covering 22 constitutionally-recognised Indian languages; component dataset BHASAANUVAAD reports ~44,400 hours; AI4Bharat targets 15,000 hours from 400+ districts; BhashaDaan crowdsources citizen voice contributions (payment terms not confirmed). [rebootdemocracy.ai](https://rebootdemocracy.ai/blog/voices-in-every-language-bhashadaan-india)

**Project Vaani (Google + IISc/ARTPARK)**: launched 2022, targeting 150,000 hours/1 million speakers across all 773 Indian districts; current release ~31,255 hours from 156,000 speakers across 165 districts and 106 languages, collected via an image-description elicitation method (speakers describe images in their own language) — methodologically the closest *elicitation design* to AMAZWI's "describe a word/concept aloud" mechanic found anywhere in this research, though Vaani is unpaid academic fieldwork, not a consumer game, and has no guessing/validation-by-peers mechanic. [vaani.iisc.ac.in](https://vaani.iisc.ac.in/)

## 3. African language AI

**Lelapa AI** (Johannesburg, founded Dec 2022) is South Africa's most visible African-language-AI startup: Vulavula (commercial transcribe/translate/analyse API) and InkubaLM (open multilingual LLM covering Swahili, Yoruba, isiXhosa, Hausa, isiZulu). It is a product/API company, not a paid-contribution data platform, and does not compete with AMAZWI's corpus-building mechanic — if anything it is a plausible *downstream buyer* of a consented SA speech corpus. [mybroadband.co.za](https://mybroadband.co.za/news/ai/637642-the-south-african-woman-who-studied-biomedical-engineering-in-japan-and-launched-tech-startup-backed-by-microsoft.html)

**Masakhane** (founded at Deep Learning Indaba 2019) is a 1,000+-member volunteer research collective, not a funded platform — it has produced 400+ models and 20+ datasets and trained 100+ African data scientists, entirely through unpaid academic-community labour. [HF masakhane](https://huggingface.co/masakhane)

**Digital Umuganda** (Rwanda, founded ~2019) collected 1,700+ hours of Kinyarwanda by May 2021 (840+ contributors), growing past 2,000 hours — the second-largest language on Common Voice after English — built around the cultural "Umuganda" (communal labour) concept; payment status for individual contributors could not be confirmed from sources found. [Mozilla Foundation](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/en/blog/mozilla-common-voice-receives-34-million-investment-to-democratize-and-diversify-voice-tech-in-east-africa/)

**Ghana NLP**: community-built Twi/Akan/Ga/Fante speech-text datasets (21,138 pairs; a separate 104,000-utterance, ~148-hour corpus purpose-built for financial-inclusion voice interfaces — directly relevant precedent for a "language tech for financial services" use case, though it is a static research dataset, not a live paid app). [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352340925006043)

**Awarri/N-ATLAS** (Nigeria): government-backed ($3.5M seed from UNDP/UNESCO/Meta/Google/Microsoft), LLM unveiled Sep 2025, focused on Yoruba/Hausa/Igbo/Ibibio/Pidgin. Its "LangEasy" platform lets "anyone with a smartphone" contribute voice/text — architecturally the closest *national-scale crowdsourcing app* precedent to AMAZWI found in Africa, though payment terms for contributors could not be confirmed and it has no guessing-game validation mechanic. [Rest of World](https://restofworld.org/2024/nigeria-awarri-ai-startup-llm/)

**Intron Health**: 16,000 hours / 3.5M clips / 18,000+ contributors / 29 countries of African-accented clinical speech, raised $1.6M pre-seed; a B2B healthtech data engine, not a consumer-facing paid-contribution game. [MSME Africa](https://msmeafricaonline.com/intron-health-secures-1-6m-to-transform-african-healthcare-with-ai/)

**Botlhale AI** (South Africa, founded 2019): enterprise conversational-AI/speech-API vendor supporting 11 SA languages (deployed e.g. in MultiChoice/DStv's WhatsApp bot) — a downstream commercial user of SA-language speech tech, not a data-collection competitor. [Botlhale AI](https://botlhale.ai/)

**DSFSI / Swivuriso (ZA-African Next Voices) — the closest thing to a direct South African precedent found.** Run by the Data Science for Social Impact group at the University of Pretoria, with Way With Words as the professional data-production/QC vendor, as South Africa's arm of the Gates-Foundation-funded ($2.2M) pan-African "African Next Voices" program (also active in Kenya and Nigeria, 9,000+ hours, 18 languages total, ~2 years, results published Oct 2025). The South African-specific release, Swivuriso, delivers **3,016 hours across 483,191 clips from 2,440 speakers in 7 SA languages** (isiZulu 500h, isiXhosa 500h, Sesotho 500h, Xitsonga 500h, Setswana 500h, isiNdebele 250h, Tshivenda 250h) spanning agriculture, healthcare, and general-domain speech. Contributors were paid, with "remuneration stratified by language to reflect engagement needs" (exact rand figures not disclosed in sources found). This is a professionally-run, funder-backed, academically-published South African speech corpus effort operating in the same time window as AMAZWI — but it is explicitly a **research data-collection exercise using a paid vendor workforce**, not a consumer game, has no public-facing app, no guessing/validation-by-strangers mechanic, and no language-learner-facing product. [DSFSI](https://www.dsfsi.co.za/za-african-next-voices/), [arXiv 2512.02201](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.02201)

**SADiLaR/NCHLT**: South Africa's oldest and most complete "all 11 languages" speech corpus (2014, ~55 hours/~200 speakers per language), built with a mix of paid part-time staff and volunteers — an internal study found paid, trained staff produced meaningfully better data than unsupervised volunteers, a relevant data point for AMAZWI's un-supervised, crowd-contributed model. SADiLaR remains the government-funded (DSI/SARIR) national infrastructure body maintaining and extending these resources. [SADiLaR](https://sadilar.org/en/), [NCHLT paper](https://www.isca-archive.org/sltu_2014/barnard14_sltu.html)

**PanSALB**: the statutory language-policy board (Act 59 of 1995); not a dataset producer, but a natural institutional partner/endorser for legitimacy. [PanSALB](https://www.pansalb.org/)

**MzansiLM/MzansiText** (UCT, 2026): a fresh (2026) 125M-parameter open language model trained on a new text corpus covering all 11 official SA languages — evidence of active, recent academic momentum in SA-language AI, though text-only, not speech. [UCT News](https://www.news.uct.ac.za/article/-2026-05-04-uct-researchers-develop-ai-model-for-11-south-african-languages)

## 4. Gamified / earn-to-contribute

**Google Crowdsource**: still alive (contrary to a common assumption that it was killed) — app updates continuing into 2026 per Wikipedia's version history. Points/badges only, no money, feeds Google Translate/Maps/Assistant. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crowdsource_(app))

**Google Local Guides**: large-scale, alive, points/levels/badges (up to 100,000 points at Level 10) — explicitly gamified but with zero monetary reward, which is the opposite design choice from AMAZWI. [SearchEngineJournal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/google-local-guides-program-adds-new-levels-new-perks/202294/)

**Duolingo Incubator (dead — important precedent)**: for roughly eight years (until Duolingo's 2021 IPO; volunteer forums formally closed March 22, 2022), Duolingo relied on unpaid volunteer translators/course-builders to create and maintain its language courses. Duolingo itself stated the reason for killing the program: once the company began monetizing courses via Duolingo Plus, continuing to extract free labour "does not feel fair and equitable," and the company also wanted to standardize course design to CEFR rather than rely on distributed volunteer judgment. **This is the single most important cautionary precedent for AMAZWI**: a large, successful crowdsourced-language platform concluded that unpaid contribution becomes untenable once the platform is making money from the contributions — validating AMAZWI's decision to pay from day one rather than retrofitting payment after monetizing. [duoplanet.com](https://duoplanet.com/what-happened-to-the-duolingo-incubator/), [Community Inc.](https://community.inc/deep-dives/contribution-duolingo)

**Duolingo isiZulu**: launched (partnered with Nal'ibali; Vodacom zero-rated the app's data for a year), confirming a large, monetized, mainstream consumer entity already teaches at least one South African language to a global audience — but as a free-standing course product with no speech-corpus-building or payment-to-speakers component. isiXhosa's live status on Duolingo could not be confirmed as of the most recent (Dec 2024) source found, and no Afrikaans course was found. [Qz.com](https://qz.com/africa/2050074/african-languages-zulu-and-xhosa-join-swahili-on-duolingo)

**Premise**: pays real cash ($0.50-$20/task; reported $40-60/week in Ghana/Nigeria) for photo/price/economic-data tasks via a gig-style app — the closest "earn small money via a phone app for real-world data contribution in the Global South" precedent outside of pure data-labeling platforms, though it collects economic/pricing data, not speech. [Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/3051471/premise-pays-app-users-to-gather-real-world-economic-data)

**DePIN/crypto**: found evidence of small-scale token-incentivized audio-quality-review micro-tasks (e.g. "DataHive AI" platform), but no evidence of a crypto-native network specifically paying for *generative* speech contribution (as opposed to reviewing existing clips) at meaningful scale. Token-based (not cash/MoMo) reward structure is a fundamentally different trust/liquidity model than AMAZWI's MoMo payout. [KuCoin](https://www.kucoin.com/blog/top-ai-depin-projects-2025-2026-decentralized-infrastructure)

**Airtel/Jio**: both building voice AI (Jio's "Native AI"/Call Agent handling 20B+ voice minutes; BharatGPT with IIT Bombay) targeting 22 Indian languages, but as commercial voice-assistant products, not open data-collection or paid-contribution initiatives — no confirmed corpus-building or contributor-payment program.

**Vodacom**: partnered with University of Johannesburg and AWS on an "AI Lab" for talent development (launched 2026), and is one of the "G6" telco coalition (with Airtel, Axian, Ethio Telecom, MTN, Orange) that announced an African-language-AI initiative at MWC Kigali in October 2025 — an announcement-stage coalition with no published dataset/hours/payment figures yet. [MTN Group](https://www.mtn.com/mtn-group-takes-up-nigerias-call-to-action-with-pledge-to-support-african-languages-research-for-ai/)

Duolingo's isiZulu course is framed around cultural context (amapiano/gqom music, minibus-taxi navigation, etc.), showing demand exists for culturally-specific SA-language learning content, but it is one-way (app teaches, user learns) with zero speech-data output or reward mechanic.

[DGMT](https://dgmt.co.za/project/nalibali/)

## 7. Games with a purpose — and the central question

**The ESP Game** (Luis von Ahn, CMU, Oct 2003): two strangers paired, shown the same image, blind to each other, race to agree on a label; 36 million labels collected by May 2008. Google licensed it as "Image Labeler" (2006), rebranded it "GWAP" in 2008, then **shut it down September 16, 2011** as part of the broader Google Labs closure — i.e., it died not because the mechanic failed but because Google deprioritized the whole experimental-products division it lived in. Briefly relaunched in 2016 in a non-game verification-only form. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ESP_game)

**reCAPTCHA v1**: repurposed bot-detection into free OCR-training/book-digitization labour at massive scale (billions of solves); **v1 was killed in March 2018** as bot-detection technology moved to invisible/behavioral scoring (v3), not because the crowdsourcing mechanic failed. The current reCAPTCHA is unrelated to games or explicit human labeling.

**Foldit** (2008-present) and **Zooniverse** (2007-present, 2.5M+ volunteers, 1 billion+ classifications) remain alive and scientifically productive, proving that "gamified citizen labour for a research corpus" is a durable model over a 15+ year horizon when the science is real and credited — a relevant existence-proof for AMAZWI's long-term viability, even though neither works with speech/audio.

**Verbosity and "Doodling"** (both academic GWAP research prototypes, mid-to-late 2000s, CMU/related labs): these are the closest *mechanically* to AMAZWI's "one person describes, another guesses, agreement = validated data" loop — Verbosity is a Taboo-style **typed**-hint/typed-guess game that harvested commonsense-fact triples; Doodling is a **drawn-image**/typed-guess game. Both prove the "describe-without-saying-it, stranger guesses, agreement validates the data" mechanic has academic precedent and works as a data-collection design — **but neither uses voice/audio in either direction**. They validate text or drawings, never spoken recordings.

Beyond these, targeted searches for a spoken/voice equivalent turned up only tangential results: a "Keep Talking and Nobody Explodes" voice-game testbed used by researchers to *evaluate* an existing ASR system (not to build/validate a corpus of contributed speech), a gamified journalism app ("J-Plus") for collecting emotionally-tagged speech for emotion-recognition research, and the Golos Russian ASR corpus, whose "validation" step is plain 5-rater majority-vote agreement on pre-recorded audio, not a live guessing game between two players. Commercial charades-style apps (Heads Up!, Voice Charades, various "Charades/Taboo" apps) exist as pure entertainment products with no research or corpus-building purpose and no evidence of speech-data extraction or ASR-training use.

**No project combining (a) one player describing a word aloud in their own language without saying it, (b) a stranger guessing by listening to that live/recorded voice, and (c) mutual agreement being used to validate the speech recording as a labeled, usable corpus item — with a cash/mobile-money reward to the speaker — was found anywhere in this research.** This does not prove such a thing has never existed anywhere (a small regional app, an unpublished hackathon project, or a shelved corporate prototype could exist without indexed English-language coverage), but an extensive, multi-angle search (GWAP literature, ASR/corpus-collection literature, party-game app stores, DePIN/crypto data networks, telco initiatives, and general web search) turned up nothing matching this specific mechanic.

## CLOSEST COMPETITOR AND THE DELTA

The single closest competitor, all factors weighted, is **DSFSI's Swivuriso / the "African Next Voices" program** (University of Pretoria, Gates Foundation-funded, Way With Words as production partner): it is the only project found that is (a) currently active, (b) specifically South African, (c) multi-language (7 official SA languages), (d) speech (not text), and (e) pays its contributors. On raw scale it is already larger than most early-stage consumer apps could hope to match quickly (3,016 hours, 2,440 speakers).

What Swivuriso does not do, and AMAZWI does:
- **No game.** Swivuriso is a managed research data-collection pipeline run through a professional vendor (Way With Words) with recruited/paid recorders and transcribers — not a self-propagating consumer app that strangers download to play with each other.
- **No stranger-validation loop.** Swivuriso's quality control is vendor-side proofing/QC, not peer agreement between two contributing strangers as the validation signal itself.
- **No dual-sided value.** Swivuriso pays speakers but gives nothing to the "listener" side — there is no listener side, because there is no guessing game. AMAZWI's guesser gets language-learning value from the same interaction that produces validated data; Swivuriso has no equivalent second beneficiary.
- **No consumer distribution mechanism.** Swivuriso is not on an app store, is not designed to spread virally as entertainment, and has no retention loop — it is a fixed-scope research deliverable (3,000 hours, then presumably winding down or moving to its next funded phase) rather than an ongoing product with compounding network effects.
- **MoMo-native payment.** Swivuriso's remuneration model (vendor-administered, "stratified by language") is a B2B payroll/vendor-payment arrangement, not an in-app, per-validated-clip, mobile-money micropayment consumer flow.

Secondary near-competitors and their gaps: **Karya** proves the "pay small amounts via phone for speech, workers retain data rights" model works at scale (35,000+ workers, India) but has no game/guessing mechanic and no South African presence found. **Awarri's LangEasy** (Nigeria) proves "anyone with a smartphone contributes voice to build a national LLM" can be a government-backed narrative, but is not gamified and not South African. **Duolingo** proves a South African language (isiZulu) can reach a mainstream global audience for learning, but produces zero speech-corpus data and pays nobody. **WAXAL** proves Google will fund large African speech corpora, but explicitly excludes South African languages as of its March 2026 release, leaving that gap open rather than contested.

## HAS THE GUESSING-GAME MECHANIC BEEN DONE?

**For text and drawings: yes** (Verbosity, Doodling — CMU GWAP research lineage, mid-2000s). **For speech/audio specifically: no confirmed instance was found.** This was searched from multiple angles — GWAP/human-computation literature, ASR/speech-corpus-collection literature, commercial party-game app stores, DePIN/crypto data-collection networks, and general web search for "voice guessing game" + "speech corpus"/"ASR"/"crowdsourcing" combinations — and none surfaced a project that uses live or recorded voice description + stranger guessing + agreement-as-validation as its data-collection mechanism. The nearest adjacent work (a bomb-defusal voice game repurposed to *evaluate* an existing ASR model, and a journalism app gamifying *emotion-tagged* speech collection) targets a different data type or a different pipeline stage (evaluation, not collection-with-validation). This is a genuine, if narrow, white space — but given how much prior art exists in adjacent forms (text GWAPs, drawing GWAPs, paid speech-collection apps, telco language-AI pushes), it should be treated as "not found despite a real search" rather than "provably first," since a small or non-English-indexed prior instance cannot be ruled out with certainty.

## GRAVEYARD: WHAT FAILED AND WHY

- **Duolingo Incubator** (unpaid volunteer course-building) — ran ~2012–2021 (IPO), forums closed March 22, 2022. Died because Duolingo started monetizing the very courses volunteers built for free and judged that continuing to take free labour was inequitable once the company profited; also moved to a standardized, centrally-controlled CEFR curriculum rather than distributed volunteer judgment. **Lesson for AMAZWI: pay from the start; don't plan to extract value from unpaid labour and add payment later.**
- **Google Image Labeler / ESP Game** (Google's licensed version) — died Sept 16, 2011, as part of the wider Google Labs shutdown, not because the guessing/agreement mechanic failed (it had already produced 36M labels). **Lesson: the mechanic's mortality risk is organizational commitment/priority, not product-market fit.**
- **reCAPTCHA v1** — killed March 2018 as anti-bot technology moved to invisible scoring; the underlying "free human labour disguised as friction" model became technically obsolete for its *primary* purpose (bot detection), even though it had been a powerful data-extraction side effect. **Lesson: don't build the core product on a mechanic (friction-based verification) that a technology shift can obsolete; AMAZWI's mechanic is intentional entertainment, not disguised friction, so this specific failure mode doesn't directly transfer, but it's a reminder that "the data collection is a side effect of something else" models are fragile if the "something else" gets solved another way.**
- **Sama/OpenAI Kenya, Scale AI/Remotasks** — not dead, but reputationally damaged: underpayment relative to client billing, payment delays/withholding, psychological harm. **Lesson: this is the reputational failure mode AMAZWI must design against from day one — transparent, fast, verifiable MoMo payment per validated clip is a direct structural answer to the "wage theft" pattern documented across this entire industry.**
- No evidence was found of a *South African* speech-corpus or language-gamification project that launched and then explicitly failed/shut down (as opposed to being superseded, still-active, or simply small). NCHLT (2014) is a completed, static, successful academic deliverable, not a failure. Mxit — the platform that once distributed Nal'ibali content — did shut down as a company, but that is a broader South African social-network death (external to language-corpus work specifically) and was not independently verified in this research pass.

## UNOCCUPIED TERRITORY

1. **The guessing-game-as-speech-validation mechanic itself** — genuinely not found elsewhere, in South Africa or globally, despite a thorough search across GWAP research, ASR/corpus literature, party-game app stores, and telco/DePIN initiatives (see above; treat as strong evidence of absence, not proof of non-existence).
2. **South African languages inside the current wave of major African-language datasets.** WAXAL (Google, 2026) confirmed excludes all SA languages. NVIDIA Granary is Europe-only. The one confirmed active SA-language speech-corpus effort (Swivuriso/African Next Voices) is a closed research pipeline with no consumer product, no virality mechanism, and (per the funding pattern) a finite grant-funded horizon rather than a self-sustaining or app-store-distributed one.
3. **A consumer-facing, self-propagating (viral/social) product for African-language speech data**, as opposed to a managed research vendor pipeline (Swivuriso) or a task-marketplace app aimed at people seeking income (Karya, Awarri's LangEasy). Every paid-speech-collection precedent found frames itself to contributors as *work*; none frame it as *play with a side benefit of pay* the way AMAZWI's game-plus-MoMo-reward structure does.
4. **Dual-sided value capture in one interaction**: paying the speaker AND teaching the guesser is not replicated by any project found — Duolingo teaches without collecting/paying, Karya/Swivuriso/WAXAL collect (and sometimes pay) without teaching the other side of the interaction.
5. **MoMo-native, telco-integrated micropayment for language data specifically** — no telco (MTN included) was found running this; MTN's own current involvement in African-language AI is a funding pledge (Sep 2025) to someone else's dataset effort (N-ATLAS), not an owned MoMo-linked product.

## Limitations
