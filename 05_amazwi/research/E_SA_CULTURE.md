# AMAZWI — South African Language, Culture & Inclusion Research

Compiled 2026-08-31. Every claim below is sourced with URL and (where available) publication date. Contested or single-source figures are flagged explicitly. This is descriptive research — it reports what sources say about race/language politics, it does not editorialise.

## 1. LANGUAGE FACTS

**Official language count — CONFIRMED: 12, not 11.**
South African Sign Language (SASL) became the 12th official language via the **Constitution Eighteenth Amendment Act, 2023**. The National Assembly approved the amendment on **2 May 2023**, and President Cyril Ramaphosa signed it into law at a ceremony at the Union Buildings on **19 July 2023**.
- [Eighteenth Amendment of the Constitution of South Africa – Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eighteenth_Amendment_of_the_Constitution_of_South_Africa)
- [The NA Approves South African Sign Language as the 12th Official Language – Parliament of South Africa](https://www.parliament.gov.za/press-releases/na-approves-south-african-sign-language-12th-official-language)
- [President Cyril Ramaphosa to enact Sign Language as 12th Official Language – The Presidency](https://www.thepresidency.gov.za/president-cyril-ramaphosa-enact-sign-language-12th-official-language)
- [South African Sign Language 12th Official Language – UCT Multilingualism Education Project, 2023-07-26](https://ched.uct.ac.za/multilingualism-education-project/articles/2023-07-26-south-african-sign-language-12th-official-language)

**Note for product framing:** the game's tagline "11 → 12 languages" is accurate but SASL is a **visual-gestural** language, not a spoken one — a "describe the word aloud, strangers guess" game mechanic cannot include it in the same modality without a video/signing mode. This is a design decision to flag, not a fact to fudge.

**English's actual rank: 5th**, spoken at home by 8.7% of the population — behind isiZulu, isiXhosa, Afrikaans, and Sepedi. This directly contradicts the common assumption (especially among non–South Africans) that English is the dominant home language; it is the dominant *public/institutional* language but a minority home language.

**Multilingualism:** [Statistics South Africa, "South Africa's Evolving Cultural Landscape: A 26-Year Transformation"](https://www.statssa.gov.za/?p=18173) reports that the proportion of **multilingual households** rose from **3.5% in 1996 to 9.4% in 2022** nationally. Provincially, Gauteng rose from 7.0% (1996) to 17.0% (2022), and Western Cape from 3.9% to 10.5% — the two highest. (Caveat: this is *household*-level multilingual reporting in the census, i.e. households where more than one language is spoken most often — this is very likely a significant undercount of individual bilingual/multilingual competence, since census "home language" methodology asks for a single primary language per person. Treat "~9%" as a conservative floor, not a ceiling, on real multilingualism.)

## 2. AFRIKAANS

**Demographic truth — CONFIRMED, with one contested figure flagged.**

The well-corroborated figures (from StatsSA's *Cultural Dynamics in South Africa* report, via WebSearch synthesis of statssa.gov.za and secondary aggregators):
- Afrikaans home-language speakers declined from 14.5% (1996) → 13.5% (2011) → **10.6% (2022)** of the total population.
- Among the **Coloured** population group, **more than 70%** report Afrikaans as home language (a ~10 percentage-point decline since 1996).
- Among **White** South Africans (7.3% of the population, Census 2022), **61%** predominantly speak Afrikaans at home.
- [The languages of South Africa – South Africa Gateway](https://southafrica-info.com/arts-culture/the-languages-of-south-africa/); [Afrikaans-speaking population of South Africa – Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afrikaans-speaking_population_of_South_Africa) (cites SAIRR projection that Coloured South Africans will continue to be the growing majority of Afrikaans speakers).

**⚠️ CONTESTED FIGURE:** A widely-circulated claim states more precisely that **"56% of Afrikaans home-language speakers are Coloured, 40% White, 4% Black African, 0.2% Indian/Asian."** This exact breakdown traces to a viral post by commentator Sandile Swana ([X/Twitter, Nov 2025](https://x.com/sandileswana/status/1995987619100991900)) citing "the 2022 census" without a direct StatsSA document link. The *directional* claim — Coloured South Africans are the numerical majority of Afrikaans home-language speakers, not White South Africans — is corroborated by StatsSA's own population-group cross-tabulation logic (Coloured population ≈ 8.2% of SA at ~70%+ Afrikaans-speaking ≈ millions more Afrikaans-Coloured speakers than the smaller 7.3% White population at 61%). Treat the **direction** of the claim (majority-Coloured) as reliable; treat the **exact 56/40/4/0.2 percentages** as unverified pending the primary StatsSA cross-tab table, which could not be extracted from the source PDF (returned corrupted on fetch — see §1). Do not put the precise 56% figure in front of a South African audience without the primary citation in hand.

**Kaaps / Afrikaaps as a distinct variety, and the dictionary movement:**
- The **Kaaps Woordeboek** (trilingual Kaaps–Afrikaans–English dictionary), compiled by University of the Western Cape linguistics professor **Quentin Williams**, launched as "the first-ever" formal dictionary of Kaaps. [The Conversation, "The first-ever dictionary of South Africa's Kaaps language has launched – why it matters"](https://theconversation.com/the-first-ever-dictionary-of-south-africas-kaaps-language-has-launched-why-it-matters-165485); [UCT News, "The importance of the first-ever Afrikaaps dictionary", 2021-08-30](https://www.news.uct.ac.za/article/-2021-08-30-the-importance-of-the-first-ever-afrikaaps-dictionary); a follow-up/expanded launch is reported at [Cape Towner, "Kaaps language gains formal recognition with the launch of its first dictionary", 2026-04-24](https://capetowner.co.za/news/2026-04-24-kaaps-language-gains-formal-recognition-with-the-launch-of-its-first-dictionary/) (note: two dictionary "launch" events are reported across 2021 and 2026 sources — likely an initial print/academic launch followed by a wider public one; both are cited here rather than reconciled, since the underlying primary announcement was not independently verified).
- Scholarly framing: Williams argues **Kaaps first cohered as a language during 16th-century intercultural encounters in the Cape** between indigenous Khoi people, traders, and later enslaved people from South East Asia and European colonisers — i.e., Kaaps is not "broken Afrikaans" but a variety with its own multi-century lineage predating standardised Afrikaans. [CIPL, "Kaaps, a language in its own right"](https://ciplnet.com/newsletter/documents/kaaps-a-language-in-its-own-right/); [Stellenbosch University thesis repository, "Afrikaaps: A celebratory protest against the racialised hegemony of 'pure' Afrikaans"](https://scholar.sun.ac.za/handle/10019.1/100364).

**Why Afrikaans matters and why it's delicate:**
- It **matters** because, factually, it is now overwhelmingly a language spoken by Black and Coloured (mostly Coloured) working- and middle-class South Africans, not primarily an Afrikaner-nationalist language — excluding it from a "SA languages" product on the assumption it is "the white/apartheid language" would itself misrepresent current demographics and exclude millions of Coloured (and a smaller number of Black) speakers.
- It is **delicate** because Afrikaans is still widely associated, in public memory and some political discourse, with apartheid-era Afrikaner nationalism (Afrikaans was the medium of the 1976 Soweto Uprising's grievance — see §7). Any treatment of Afrikaans needs to visibly represent **Kaaps/Coloured Afrikaans** alongside "standard" Afrikaans, or it will read as re-centring the historically dominant register.

## 3. CODE-SWITCHING AND URBAN VARIETIES

**Tsotsitaal / Iscamtho:** Urban youth vernaculars spoken predominantly (historically) by males in Black urban townships. Key linguistic distinction from academic literature: **Tsotsitaal** uses a **non-standard Afrikaans matrix** (historically strongest in Gauteng/Pretoria/older township contexts), while **Iscamtho** uses a **Bantu-language matrix (usually Zulu)**, especially in Soweto/Johannesburg contexts. Both are also called Flaaitaal or Ringas in various cities. [Cambridge, "Tsotsitaals, Urban Vernaculars and Contact Linguistics"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/youth-language-practices-and-urban-language-contact-in-africa/tsotsitaals-urban-vernaculars-and-contact-linguistics/44961370B767E87461AC651A54694B37); [ResearchGate, "Overview of the Tsotsitaals of South Africa; their different base languages and common core lexical items"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/309291537_OVERVIEW_OF_THE_TSOTSITAALS_OF_SOUTH_AFRICA_THEIR_DIFFERENT_BASE_LANGUAGES_AND_COMMON_CORE_LEXICAL_ITEMS).

**Sepitori** ("Pretoria Sotho"): a contact variety in Tshwane blending Setswana/Sepedi with other township-language input; academic work frames it as "a salient urban marker" distinguishing insiders from outsiders and argues it should be "institutionally recorded, formalised and supported" rather than dismissed as slang. [Academia.edu, "Language contact in African urban settings: The case of Sepitori in Tshwane"](https://www.academia.edu/29715194/Language_contact_in_African_urban_settings_The_case_of_Sepitori_in_Tshwane).

**Sabela:** originated in early-1900s mine compounds and became the secret language of South Africa's prison "Numbers Gangs" (26/27/28); distinct from but related to Tsotsitaal — described in sources as maintained "in slightly different forms as a prison language and among Afrikaans-speaking Black communities." [afrikaiswoke.com, "Secret Language Of South Africa's Numbers Gang"](https://www.afrikaiswoke.com/secret-language-of-south-africas-numbers-gang/); [Wikipedia, "Tsotsitaal and Camtho"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsotsitaal_and_Camtho).

**Kasi-taal:** used in sources largely interchangeably with township/kasi slang broadly (kasi = location/township); less academically distinct than Tsotsitaal/Iscamtho/Sepitori as a named linguistic system in the literature surfaced here — treat as a popular umbrella term rather than a linguistically bounded variety with its own dedicated academic corpus.

**Why linguistically valuable and absent from datasets:** the special issue *"Tsotsitaal studies: urban youth language practices in South Africa"* frames these varieties as sites of "individual creativity and stylistic choice" and identity performance central to urban Black South African youth culture — yet, being non-standardised, orally transmitted, rapidly evolving, and historically stigmatised as "broken" or criminal-associated (Sabela) speech, **none of them have institutional orthographies, and none appear in mainstream ASR/NLP training data**, which is built overwhelmingly from standardised, written, formally-registered language (news text, Bibles, parliamentary proceedings). [Academia.edu, "Special Issue: Tsotsitaal studies"](https://www.academia.edu/9914332/Special_Issue_Tsotsitaal_studies_urban_youth_language_practices_in_South_Africa). A game mechanic built around *spoken, informal, everyday* word descriptions is structurally well-suited to capturing exactly this register that formal corpora miss — this is a genuine, defensible differentiator, not just marketing.

## 4. ENDANGERED LANGUAGES

**N|uu:**
- Widely reported as **the most endangered language in South Africa**, from the ǂKhomani San community, Northern Cape.
- **Katrina Esau ("Ouma Katrina")** is consistently reported across sources (UCT, France24 Oct 2025, Wikipedia as of its 2026-08-19 update) as the **last fluent first-language speaker**, aged 92–93 (b. 1933), and **alive** as of the most recent sources checked. [France24, "Last fluent speaker on a quest to keep ancient S.African language alive", 2025-10-10](https://www.france24.com/en/live-news/20251010-last-fluent-speaker-on-a-quest-to-keep-ancient-s-african-language-alive); [Wikipedia, "Katrina Esau"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katrina_Esau) (page states she is "the last living native speaker"; historically, ~20 fluent speakers were identified in the 1990s, all aged 60+ at that time).
- **Honours confirmed:** Order of the Baobab (Silver), **2014**, "for her excellent contribution to the preservation of a language that is facing a threat of extinction" — [The Presidency, "Katrina Esau"](https://www.thepresidency.gov.za/katrina-esau); Honorary Doctorate from the **University of Cape Town, 2023**, "in recognition for her efforts to the preservation of the Nǀuu language and oral literature" — [UCT News, "Ouma Katrina Esau: Reviving a language, one child at a time", 2023-03-31](https://www.news.uct.ac.za/article/-2023-03-31-ouma-katrina-esau-reviving-a-language-one-child-at-a-time).
- **⚠️ Flag:** the Wikipedia infobox reportedly also lists a further Order of the Baobab entry dated 2025; this could not be independently corroborated in the time available and may be a data artifact — do not cite a 2025 re-award without confirming against the Presidency's official honours list.
- She runs a school, "Staar na die Sterre" ("Gazing at the Stars"), teaching N|uu to children with materials developed 2012–2016 by UCT's **Centre for African Language Diversity (CALDi)**, including the first illustrated N|uu-Afrikaans-English reader produced with ǂKhomani community members in Upington. [UCT Faculty of Humanities, "New language tool for SA's most endangered language"](https://humanities.uct.ac.za/articles/2016-03-18-new-language-tool-sas-most-endangered-language).

**Khoekhoegowab (Nama):** ~167,000 speakers as of 2020 per one source cited in search; ~50,900 speakers of Nama specifically as of 2020 per another. It is the one Khoisan-family language with a degree of formal recognition/support in South Africa (spoken more widely in Namibia). Community-based revitalisation work is documented in the Northern Cape. [Community-based Revitalisation of Nama in the Northern Cape – NWU Journals](https://journals.nwu.ac.za/index.php/ijilmd/article/view/570). (Note: the ~167,000 vs ~50,900 figures appear to describe different scopes — likely regional/national Nama-speaker counts vs. all Khoekhoegowab speakers or a different year/region; both numbers came from a single aggregated search summary and should be treated as approximate pending the primary demographic source.)

**ǂKhomani San / Hugh Brody Collection:** the ǂKhomani San community (Northern Cape) is where N|uu survives; a **ǂKhomani | Hugh Brody Archive** was digitised and went live in **March 2024**, funded by SADiLaR, housed at UCT Special Collections. [Academia.edu, "Endangered African Languages Featured in a Digital Collection: The Case of the ǂKhomani San, Hugh Brody Collection"](https://www.academia.edu/77572959/); [CLARIN ERIC, "Digital Preservation of Endangered Languages in South Africa"](https://www.clarin.eu/impact-stories/digital-preservation-endangered-languages-south-africa).

**PanSALB's role:** PanSALB is the constitutional body tasked with protecting/promoting all 12 official languages, "especially all the marginalised indigenous South African languages, including the Khoi, Nama, San and South African Sign Languages." It runs **community language schools in the Northern Cape, Eastern Cape, Gauteng and Western Cape** and, in September 2024, hosted a **"Khoe and San National Language Indaba"** as part of aligning with the **UNESCO International Decade of Indigenous Languages (2022–2032)**. [dfa.co.za, "PanSALB hosts Khoe and San language indaba", 2024-09-03](https://dfa.co.za/news/2024-09-03-pansalb-hosts-khoe-and-san-language-indaba/); [Wikipedia, "Pan South African Language Board"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_South_African_Language_Board). PanSALB has also partnered with Briza Publications on an audio-visual N|uu dictionary. [PanSALB, "The survival of African languages in a digital era"](https://www.pansalb.org/the-survival-of-african-languages-in-a-digital-era/). A specific "PanSALB endangered-language list" as a standalone published document was not directly located in this research pass — PanSALB's public materials name Khoi, Nama, San languages and SASL as priority/marginalised languages rather than publishing a single ranked list; do not assert a specific list exists without further verification.

## 5. ORAL TRADITION

**Terminology (confirmed):**
- **Folktales:** *izinganekwane* (isiZulu), *iintsomi* (isiXhosa).
- **Praise poetry:** *izibongo* (Nguni languages), *direto* (Sotho-Tswana languages); the praise-poet performer is the **imbongi**.
- **Proverbs:** *izaga* (Nguni), ***maele*** (Sesotho).
- **Riddles:** *dilotho* (Sesotho).
Source: [Sesotho Web, "Proverbs, idioms and riddles"](http://www.sesotho.web.za/ref1.htm); [Wikipedia, "Izibongo"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Izibongo) / [Wikipedia, "Imbongi"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imbongi).

**Scholarly framing:** Southern African oral literature is conventionally grouped into three genres — **oral poetry** (praise poems/songs), **narrative** (folktales, myths, legends, fables), and **wisdom-lore** (idioms, riddles, proverbs). [Britannica, "African literature – Riddles, Oral Traditions, Proverbs"](https://www.britannica.com/art/African-literature/The-riddle). Izibongo scholarship (Jeff Opland and others) argues the genre is broader than "praise" — it can contain **criticism**, not just flattery, and remains under-studied via fieldwork despite its cultural centrality. [Wikipedia, "Izibongo"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Izibongo); a 1997 *SA Journal of Folklore Studies* piece on "Xhosa Oral Poetry and its Reception" notes the tradition persists but few fieldwork-based analytical studies exist. Riddles are documented as pedagogical tools that transmit cultural values across generations, particularly among children. [ResearchGate, "Riddles, meanings and cognitive development of the African child in the siSwati tradition"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/348447789).

**What is being lost:** scholarly and popular sources consistently frame oral tradition (izibongo, folktales, proverbs) as endangered by urbanisation, the decline of intergenerational oral transmission, and formal schooling's privileging of written/English literacy over home-language oral performance — this is the recurring subtext of essentially every source above, though a single dedicated "decline study" was not pulled out individually in this pass.

**Existing digital archives:**
1. **The Digital Bleek and Lloyd** (UCT) — digitised 19th-century notebooks documenting ǀxam and !kun (San) language, folklore, and culture: 114 Lucy Lloyd ǀxam notebooks, 13 Lloyd !kun notebooks, 28 Wilhelm Bleek ǀxam notebooks, 32 Dorothea Bleek notebooks, plus watercolours; digitisation began 2003; includes a 280,000-word searchable index. Housed jointly across the National Library of SA, Iziko SA Museum, UCT, and Unisa, coordinated by the **Lucy Lloyd Archive, Resource and Exhibition Centre (LLAREC)** at UCT's Centre for Curating the Archive. [digitalbleeklloyd.uct.ac.za](https://digitalbleeklloyd.uct.ac.za/); [UCT Centre for Curating the Archive](https://humanities.uct.ac.za/cca/collections/digital-bleek-and-lloyd). A newer iteration, **"ǃkhwe ta ǀxōë"**, has also launched. [Varsity Newspaper, "Launching ǃkhwe ta ǀxōë, the New Digital Bleek and Lloyd"](https://varsitynewspaper.substack.com/p/launching-khwe-ta-xoe-the-new-digital).
2. **SADiLaR** (South African Centre for Digital Language Resources) — national centre, founded 2016, hosted at North-West University, funded by the Department of Science, Technology and Innovation; builds/distributes digital language resources and corpora across all 12 official languages for research use, including documentation of cultural-heritage practices as "a living archive." [sadilar.org](https://sadilar.org/en/); [Wikipedia, "SADiLaR"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SADiLaR).
3. **Ulwazi Programme** — a community-generated indigenous-knowledge wiki set up by the eThekwini Municipal Library and Heritage Department (Durban); trains volunteer community fieldworkers in oral-history methods; content (oral histories, family histories, rites-of-passage/ceremony knowledge) accessible via 89 public libraries across KwaZulu-Natal and online. [ulwaziprogramme.org](https://www.ulwaziprogramme.org/); [Springer, "The Ulwazi Programme: A Case Study in Community-Focused Indigenous Knowledge Management"](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4899-7392-4_13).
4. **ǂKhomani | Hugh Brody Archive** (UCT Special Collections, live March 2024, SADiLaR-funded) — see §4.

## 6. LANGUAGE LEARNING DEMAND IN SA

**Incremental Introduction of African Languages (IIAL) — school policy:** Department of Basic Education policy (approved 2013) requiring all non-African-home-language learners to be incrementally taught an African language, Grade 1 through Grade 12, framed explicitly around **social cohesion** and reversing the historical marginalisation of African languages. Roll-out began with a 2014 Grade 1 pilot, and was to be phased in **year-by-year through to Grade 12 in 2026** (i.e., the final cohort reaches full IIAL exposure this year). As of 2017, **27% of public schools** nationally were implementing IIAL in Grades 1–2; the Department's target is **3,558 public schools across all grades by 2029**. [education.gov.za, IIAL Policy, September 2013 PDF](https://www.education.gov.za/Portals/0/Documents/Policies/IIAL%20Policy%20September%202013%20(3).pdf); [gov.za, "Incremental Introduction of African Languages policy", 2013-11-13](https://www.gov.za/news/media-statements/incremental-introduction-african-languages-policy-13-nov-2013).

**Duolingo:** offers **Zulu** (added August 2022, launched in partnership with literacy NGO **Nal'ibali**, with **Vodacom zero-rating** the app for a year in South Africa to widen access) and **Swahili** among African languages. **Xhosa was available but discontinued around September 2023** "due to low user engagement and limited resources for course development." **Afrikaans is not offered** by Duolingo. [Duolingo Blog, "Welcome Zulu to the (language) family!"](https://blog.duolingo.com/welcome-zulu-to-the-language-family/); [Quartz Africa, "South Africa's most popular languages are coming to Duolingo"](https://qz.com/africa/2050074/african-languages-zulu-and-xhosa-join-swahili-on-duolingo); [duolingoguides.com, "Duolingo Xhosa: Current Status And Alternatives"](https://duolingoguides.com/duolingo-xhosa/). This is a directly relevant market signal: **the world's largest language-learning platform tried a South African language, kept only one (Zulu) of two, and has never offered Afrikaans** — evidence that generic global language-app models under-serve SA languages and there is room (and a documented gap) for a locally-built alternative.

**Existing SA-specific apps and traction:** several smaller apps exist — **Puo**, **Learn Zulu Fast**, **uTalk (Zulu)**, **Angula** (covers isiNdebele, isiXhosa, isiZulu and others) — but none surfaced in this research with independently reported install/traction numbers; they appear to be niche/low-visibility relative to Duolingo. No dedicated market-research report quantifying SA demand for South Africans to learn each other's home languages (as opposed to English/foreign languages) was located in this pass — this is a genuine research gap, not a confirmed absence, and is worth a follow-up targeted search before citing "no such research exists" as a fact.

**Directly relevant prior art — a speech-data-collection precedent already exists:** **Swivuriso**, the **"South African Next Voices"** multilingual speech dataset, is a 2026 academic project (Data Science for Social Impact / DSFSI group, 22+ authors) that collected South African-language speech data and released it under a **CC BY 4.0** licence, with code/data at [github.com/dsfsi/za-african-next-voices](https://github.com/dsfsi/za-african-next-voices). [arXiv 2512.02201, "Swivuriso: The South African Next Voices Multilingual Speech Dataset"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.02201). This is the closest existing comparable to AMAZWI's "consented speech archive" ambition and should be reviewed directly (methodology, consent language, licensing choice) before AMAZWI finalises its own data-collection consent flow — it is evidence both that there is academic appetite for exactly this kind of corpus, and that a credible ethical/licensing template already exists to benchmark against.

## 7. CULTURAL CALENDAR

- **Heritage Month = September; Heritage Day = 24 September — CONFIRMED.** Public holiday under the Public Holidays Act (Act No. 36 of 1994). First celebrated 24 September 1995; the specific date was reportedly a political **compromise** during 1994 negotiations (an earlier proposal to name the day after a specific figure/group was replaced with a day "that belonged to everyone," covering the full breadth of SA's cultural inheritance). [Wikipedia, "Heritage Day (South Africa)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heritage_Day_(South_Africa)); [gauteng.net, "What Is Heritage Day? The History Behind South Africa's National Celebration"](https://www.gauteng.net/whats-on-g/what-is-heritage-day-the-history/).
- **Youth Day = 16 June — CONFIRMED.** Commemorates the **1976 Soweto Uprising**, in which a student-led demonstration (triggered specifically by the enforced use of **Afrikaans** as a medium of instruction) was met with police violence, sparking nationwide unrest. [districtsix.co.za, "Youth Day 16 June"](https://www.districtsix.co.za/youth-day-16-june/); [nmsa.org.za, "Origins of Youth Day in South Africa"](https://www.nmsa.org.za/news/10-news/226-origins-of-youth-day-in-south-africa.html). **Product-relevant nuance:** Youth Day's origin is *itself* a language-policy grievance (forced Afrikaans instruction) — this is historically loaded territory for any product foregrounding Afrikaans, and argues strongly for the earlier point that Afrikaans content must visibly centre Kaaps/Coloured and Black Afrikaans speakers, not the "standard"/historically-imposed register.
- **International Mother Language Day = 21 February — CONFIRMED.** UNESCO-proclaimed global observance "to promote awareness of linguistic and cultural diversity and to promote multilingualism." ## 8. DATA ETHICS AND POLITICS

**Data colonialism (foundational theory):** Nick Couldry and Ulises Mejias, in *The Costs of Connection* (2019) and the related article "Data Colonialism: Rethinking Big Data's Relation to the Contemporary Subject" (*Television & New Media*, 2019), argue that mass data extraction constitutes **"a new stage of colonialism based on appropriating human life itself... made valuable in the form of data,"** combining historical extractive traditions with contemporary data science to exploit data and labour for profit, disproportionately benefiting Global North corporations/states. [SAGE Journals, Couldry & Mejias 2019](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1527476418796632); [LSE Research Online, accepted manuscript PDF](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/89511/1/Couldry_Data-colonialism_Accepted.pdf); applied to an African context specifically in [Research ICT Africa, "Colonising ourselves? An introduction to data colonialism"](https://researchictafrica.net/2020/03/23/colonising-ourselves-an-introduction-to-data-colonialism/). Note: the thesis has itself been critiqued in the literature for "data universalism" — glossing over important local specificities — per [Taylor & Francis, "The decolonial turn in data and technology research"](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1369118X.2021.1986102).

**Masakhane's position:** Masakhane is a grassroots, pan-African, open-source NLP research effort whose explicit mission is strengthening African-language NLP **"for Africans, by Africans."** Research emerging from/about the Masakhane community frames the core concern as **data sovereignty, community ownership, and ethical data practices** as the corrective to "asymmetrical power relations that underpin data colonialism," and the community is actively debating **whether existing copyright/privacy law is even adequate** to capture questions of agency and community ownership once data is made open. [ResearchGate, "Decolonizing African NLP: A Survey on Power Dynamics and Data Colonialism in Tech Development"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/379815378); [Carnegie Endowment, "How African NLP Experts Are Navigating the Challenges of Copyright, Innovation, and Access", 2024-04](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2024/04/how-african-nlp-experts-are-navigating-the-challenges-of-copyright-innovation-and-access).

**AU Data Policy Framework:** Published by the African Union on **28 July 2022** (endorsed by the AU Executive Council in February 2022). Explicitly frames **data sovereignty as a principle it supports**, while deliberately **separating it from "data localization"** justified on security grounds — i.e., the AU's own framework distinguishes "African control over African data" from "African data must physically never leave Africa," which is a more nuanced position than either extreme. The stated rationale: sovereignty over data lets governments and businesses "develop context-specific data sets and policies... while having the chance to identify and remedy any unjustifiable biases." [au.int, "AU Data Policy Framework"](https://au.int/en/documents/20220728/au-data-policy-framework); [CIPESA, "Key Takeaways From the 2022 African Union Data Policy Framework"](https://cipesa.org/2022/11/key-takeaways-from-the-2022-african-union-data-policy-framework/); [Future of Privacy Forum summary](https://fpf.org/blog/the-african-unions-data-policy-framework-context-key-takeaways-and-implications-for-data-protection-on-the-continent/).

**Te Mana Raraunga (Māori Data Sovereignty Network) — as a model:** publishes explicit **Principles of Māori Data Sovereignty** organised under four Māori concepts: **Rangatiratanga** (authority — control, jurisdiction, self-determination over Māori data), **Whakapapa** (relationships — context, disaggregation, future use), **Whanaungatanga** (obligations — balancing rights and accountabilities), **Kotahitanga** (collective benefit — benefit-sharing, capacity-building, connection). Notably, the principles state data should, "whenever possible," be **stored in Aotearoa New Zealand** for the benefit of current and future generations. [Te Mana Raraunga, "Principles of Māori Data Sovereignty"](https://www.temanararaunga.maori.nz/principles-of-maori-data-sovereignty) (PDF: [Oct 2018 principles document](https://cdn.auckland.ac.nz/assets/psych/about/our-research/documents/TMR+M%C4%81ori+Data+Sovereignty+Principles+Oct+2018.pdf)). This is a directly reusable framework for AMAZWI: an equivalent "who has authority over the archive, what obligations exist to contributors, who benefits, where is it stored" checklist, localised to South African languages/communities, would let AMAZWI make a defensible affirmative claim rather than just avoiding bad press.

**What makes a corpus ethically defensible vs. extractive** (synthesised across the African-NLP-ethics literature surfaced): defensible practice consistently includes (a) **explicit, revocable, informed consent** at the point of contribution, covering downstream uses (research, model training, redistribution); (b) **transparent provenance** — web-scraped or repurposed data without consent is flagged across multiple sources as "ethically problematic"; (c) **value return to the community** — via released tools, revenue-share, or community-controlled governance of the resulting asset, not just extraction into a proprietary model; (d) **participatory design** of the collection process itself, not just after-the-fact consent to someone else's design. [waywithwords.net, "Ethics in African Language Speech Collection"](https://waywithwords.net/blog/speech-data-collection-african-languages"); [arXiv, "The African Languages Lab: A Collaborative Approach to Advancing Low-Resource African NLP"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.05644); ResearchGate "Decolonizing African NLP" (cited above).

## 9. INCLUSIVE DESIGN IN SA

**Adult literacy rate:** UNESCO/World Bank data puts South Africa's **adult (15+) literacy rate at ~90%** (male 91%, female 89%, ~2021 data) — well above the Sub-Saharan African average of ~68.2% (2023). [World Bank Data, "Literacy rate, adult total... South Africa"](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/SE.ADT.LITR.ZS?locations=ZA); [Statista, "South Africa - Literacy rate"](https://www.statista.com/statistics/572836/literacy-rate-in-south-africa/).

**⚠️ Contested/complicating figure — functional reading literacy is a different, much worse number.** The **PIRLS 2021** international reading assessment found **81% of South African Grade 4 learners could not read for meaning in any language** (up from 78% in 2016); South Africa ranked **last of 43 participating countries**, with the largest score decline of any country, and one projection cited suggests it will take **more than 80 years** at current trajectory before 95% of Grade 4 learners can read for meaning. [Equal Education / joint statement on PIRLS 2021](https://equaleducation.org.za/joint-statement-the-2021-progress-in-international-reading-literacy-study-pirls-results-confirm-a-schooling-system-in-crisis-and-the-extent-of-learning-losses-created-by-covid-19/); [SAHRC media statement on PIRLS 2021](https://www.sahrc.org.za/index.php/sahrc-media/news-2/item/3589-media-statement-progress-in-international-reading-literacy-study-pirls-2021-results); [Right to Read campaign, "South Africa has a literacy crisis"](https://www.righttoread.org.za/why/). **These two figures are not contradictory** — "can sign your name / read a simple everyday sentence" (basic/UNESCO literacy, ~90%) and "can read a Grade 4-level text for comprehension" (PIRLS functional literacy, ~19%) measure very different things. For a game whose mechanic is *reading a word clue and describing it, or hearing a description and guessing*, this gap matters directly: a **voice-first / audio-first design** (speaking and listening rather than reading dense text) is not just "more South African," it is more accessible given the actual measured reading-comprehension distribution — this is a concrete, source-backed design argument, not just a stylistic preference.

**Multilingual brand communication:** general design commentary on the South African market states that "inclusive and sustainable design is crucial in South Africa, where design cannot ignore context, including whether packaging is readable in 11 [now 12] languages" — i.e., multilingual accessibility is treated as a baseline design requirement in the local design discourse, not an optional nicety. [Jambo Africa Online, "The importance and meaning of design in brand management"](https://www.jamboafrica.online/25070-2/). MTN and Vodacom are consistently reported as South Africa's two most valuable brands (MTN R68.2bn, Vodacom R43.9bn per one 2024 ranking), with Nando's also ranked among the most iconic/valuable — useful context for AMAZWI as an MTN MoMo-adjacent product operating alongside brands with genuinely mass, cross-demographic reach. [Bizcommunity, "MTN maintains top position in Brand Finance South Africa 100 report"](https://www.bizcommunity.com/article/mtn-maintains-top-position-in-brand-finance-south-africa-100-report-as-vodacom-narrows-the-gap-786692a). No single authoritative case study on "how SA brands specifically solve multilingual UX" was located in this pass beyond this general design-context statement — this is a gap worth a dedicated follow-up (e.g., studying Vodacom/MTN's own IVR and USSD language-selection menus, which are a real, ubiquitous, already-solved SA example of multilingual mass-market UX, but were not directly sourced here).

**Disability/inclusion note:** SASL's 2023 constitutional recognition (§1) is itself the clearest current national signal on disability-inclusive language policy; no additional disability-specific inclusive-design research beyond that was gathered in this pass given time constraints — flagged as an area for a follow-up pass focused specifically on accessibility (screen-reader support, deaf/hard-of-hearing accommodation given SASL is visual-gestural and the core game mechanic is audio-based — a direct design tension, see product ideas below).

## TEN AUTHENTICALLY SOUTH AFRICAN PRODUCT IDEAS THIS RESEARCH SUGGESTS

1. **"Kasi Mode"** — a dedicated game mode for Tsotsitaal/Iscamtho/Sepitori words, explicitly framed as *documenting* living township vernacular (not "slang trivia"), positioned as the one place these varieties get taken seriously as language rather than as comic relief.
2. **Kaaps-inclusive Afrikaans, not "standard Afrikaans only"** — split Afrikaans content into Kaaps and standard registers from day one, crediting Kaaps as its own variety (per the UWC Kaaps Woordeboek precedent), so the app doesn't accidentally reproduce the "impure vs pure Afrikaans" hierarchy the Kaaps movement is fighting.
3. **A ring-fenced Khoisan/N|uu preservation track** with direct pathways to PanSALB, CALDi (UCT), and Katrina Esau's school — treat this not as "one more language" but as a visible, time-sensitive preservation mission (N|uu has one remaining first-language speaker), with proceeds/credit flowing back to that community specifically.
4. **Oral-tradition rounds** — izibongo/direto (praise-poetry), izinganekwane/iintsomi (folktales), izaga/maele (proverbs) and riddles as a distinct clue category, explicitly citing and linking outward to the Digital Bleek and Lloyd archive, SADiLaR, and Ulwazi Programme rather than treating this as generic "culture trivia."
5. **Provincial/home-turf leaderboards**, using the actual Census 2022 provincial dominance patterns (isiXhosa Eastern Cape, isiZulu KZN/Gauteng, Sesotho Free State, Afrikaans/isiXhosa/English Western Cape) so competition feels locally grounded rather than generically "national."
6. **Voice-first, minimal-reading UI** — given the PIRLS 81% functional-reading-comprehension gap, design the core loop around speaking/listening rather than dense on-screen text, which is both more inclusive and more "oral culture"-authentic.
7. **A visible Te Mana Raraunga–style data charter**, localised — publish, in plain language, who owns a contributor's recorded voice clip, how it can be revoked, what it's used for, and what the contributor gets back — modelled on the AU Data Policy Framework's sovereignty-without-localism-extremism stance and Masakhane's community-ownership position, cited as such.
8. **IIAL-aligned "learn a language, not just guess it" mode** — since the Incremental Introduction of African Languages policy reaches its final Grade 12 cohort in 2026, a structured beginner-learner track (vs. pure guessing-game) targeted at exactly the age group living through IIAL, and at workplace adult learners, fills the gap Duolingo left when it dropped Xhosa and never added Afrikaans.
9. **Heritage Month (September) as the annual flagship live event**, with Heritage Day (24 September) as a finale, and a smaller Youth Day (16 June) feature that explicitly and respectfully acknowledges the day's origin in a language-policy grievance (forced Afrikaans instruction) rather than treating it as a generic "youth" day.
10. **A rewards structure that pays speakers, not just players** — since MTN MoMo rewards are the retention hook, make explicit that description-clip *contributors* (the people whose speech becomes the corpus) earn differently/additionally from *guessers*, operationalising "speakers earn MoMo rewards" as a distinct, visible revenue line rather than folding it into general gameplay rewards.

## WHAT WOULD MAKE THIS LOOK EXTRACTIVE — AND THE FIX FOR EACH

1. **Extractive:** Collecting voice/speech data under vague "by playing you agree to our terms" consent, then using it to train or sell a commercial ASR/NLP model without a separate, specific opt-in.
   **Fix:** Layered consent — one for "voice used to power gameplay for you," a separate explicit opt-in for "voice may be used in an anonymised research/training corpus," each revocable independently, per Masakhane's "existing copyright/privacy frameworks may not be sufficient" caution (§8) and Te Mana Raraunga's Rangatiratanga principle.

2. **Extractive:** Building the endangered-language (N|uu, Nama) content without involving PanSALB, CALDi, or the ǂKhomani/Nama communities themselves — i.e., "featuring" an endangered language as content without the community's participation or benefit.
   **Fix:** Direct partnership/attribution/revenue-share with the actual preservation bodies named in §4 (PanSALB, UCT CALDi, Katrina Esau's school) before shipping any N|uu content; treat it as a partnership, not a content category.

3. **Extractive:** Treating Tsotsitaal/Iscamtho/Sepitori as "fun slang" content for outsiders to laugh at, without crediting it as a real linguistic system or compensating/crediting the township communities whose speech patterns generate the content.
   **Fix:** Frame explicitly as documentation of living language varieties (citing the academic literature in §3), with in-app attribution, and ideally paid or credited contribution from actual speakers of these varieties, not just harvesting the words.

4. **Extractive:** Using the "56% of Afrikaans speakers are Coloured" statistic (or Afrikaans-Coloured identity generally) as a marketing hook/talking point without engaging any Coloured Afrikaans-speaking community in product decisions — using the demographic fact as content while excluding the community from the room.
   **Fix:** Verify the exact statistic against a primary StatsSA source before using it publicly (see §2 flag), and ensure Coloured/Kaaps-speaking team members or advisors are actually involved in decisions about how Afrikaans content is presented — don't just cite the demography, reflect it in who builds the product.

5. **Extractive:** Storing all contributor voice data on infrastructure/servers outside South Africa with no disclosure, monetising a "South African" data asset for a foreign-headquartered beneficiary with no local benefit-sharing.
   **Fix:** Apply the AU Data Policy Framework's sovereignty framing (§8) — disclose where data lives, and ensure some concrete local benefit (MoMo rewards, community fund, open dataset release under a Masakhane-style community licence) flows back, rather than the value flowing one way outward.

6. **Extractive:** Publishing a folktale, praise poem, or proverb sourced from an existing archive (e.g. Digital Bleek and Lloyd, Ulwazi) without attribution, as if AMAZWI generated the content itself.
   **Fix:** Explicit sourcing/attribution to the originating archive and, where the archive requests it, to the named narrator/community, every time (this is standard practice already followed by the archives themselves, per their own site descriptions in §5).

## LANDMINES: what a South African judge would find offensive or naive

1. **Treating "11 languages became 12" as a marketing beat without acknowledging SASL is a signed, visual-gestural language** that a spoken-word guessing game structurally cannot include on equal footing — claiming full inclusion of "all 12 official languages" while SASL gets a token/absent treatment will read as tokenism to anyone who knows the 2023 constitutional history (§1).

2. **Presenting Afrikaans as "the Afrikaner language"** or, conversely, over-correcting into treating it as purely a Coloured-community language and sidelining white Afrikaans speakers — either framing misrepresents the actual mixed demographic reality (§2) and will read as either historically ignorant or as erasure, depending on which way it tips.

3. **Using standard/"pure" Afrikaans as the default and treating Kaaps as a bonus/novelty variant** rather than the reverse or at least an equal footing — given Kaaps speakers have spent decades fighting exactly this hierarchy (§2), a product that reproduces it, even unintentionally through simple defaults, will land badly with the Kaaps/Cape Flats community specifically.

4. **Featuring Youth Day (16 June) content that doesn't acknowledge its origin in the 1976 anti-Afrikaans-imposition uprising** — a generic "celebrate youth!" treatment of a day whose entire historical meaning is a language-policy protest, especially inside a language-focused app, would be read as either ignorant or evasive (§7).

5. **Commercialising N|uu or other Khoisan-language content without the ǂKhomani/San community's direct involvement and benefit** — with one first-language N|uu speaker alive, any perception of a commercial app "harvesting" this specific, named, elderly, honoured individual's linguistic heritage for engagement/rewards without her community's direct partnership would be a severe and specific reputational risk, not a generic one (§4).

6. **Publishing the unverified "56%/40%/4%/0.2%" Afrikaans-by-race breakdown** as if it were an official StatsSA figure — a South African judge steeped in census methodology would immediately ask for the primary source, and citing an unverified social-media statistic as demographic fact is a credibility risk independent of the politics (§2).

7. **A "learn South African languages" pitch that ignores the PIRLS 81% functional-illiteracy figure** and assumes a text-heavy, read-the-clue UX will work for the full population it claims to include — a judge familiar with SA's actual education data would see a text-first design as evidence the team didn't do basic homework on who they're building for (§9).

8. **Treating township/prison-originated varieties (Tsotsitaal, Sabela) as fun content without acknowledging Sabela's origin in the Numbers Gangs and mine-compound coercive labour history** — presenting it as cute slang without any acknowledgment of that origin risks looking either uninformed or exploitative of a genuinely difficult history (§3).

9. **A rewards/archive structure where the fine print lets AMAZWI (or MTN) retain unrestricted rights to resell or license the voice corpus**, discovered later by a journalist or researcher — given the live, active "data colonialism" debate within African NLP circles specifically about extractive corpus-building (§8), this is the single most likely way the product ends up as a negative case study rather than a positive one.

10. **Marketing the product as "unmistakably South African" while the founding/build team has no demonstrable representation of the language communities being featured** (isiZulu, isiXhosa, Sepedi, Setswana, Sesotho, Tsonga, Swati, Venda, Ndebele, Khoisan, SASL, Kaaps-Afrikaans) — authenticity claims invite exactly this scrutiny, and a judge would likely ask directly "who on your team speaks these languages" before evaluating anything else.
